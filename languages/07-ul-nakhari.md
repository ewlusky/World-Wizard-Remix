## Cultural Context

The Ul'nakhari are the great ocean-navigators of the world — island-hoppers, deep-sea fishers, and traders who cross open ocean by reading stars, current, and wind. Their language reflects their world: almost no friction consonants (no f, v, s, z), flowing vowel sequences, and an elaborate directional system that encodes movement relative to shore. They have six directional particles where English has two (to/from): toward deep water, toward shore, along the coast, across the current, against the current, and upward/downward.

## Phonology & Grammar Rules

1. Consonants are severely limited to: m, n, ng (as one sound), k, l, h, p, w, and the glottal stop (written '). No fricatives (no f, v, s, sh, th, z).

2. Vowels are a, e, i, o, u. All vowels may be lengthened (written double: aa, ee, ii, oo, uu). Adjacent vowels are each pronounced separately: kai = "kah-ee", not "ky."

3. Syllable structure is strictly CV (consonant-vowel) or V (vowel alone). No consonant clusters. No syllable-final consonants.

4. The six directional particles (always placed after the verb): maka (toward deep water), uka (toward shore), kahakai (along coast), kua (against current), holo (with current), luna/lalo (up/down).

5. Possession: kā (my/ours), kō (your), kona (their). "My canoe" = kā wa'a; "the ocean's gift" = kona moana-hāwai.

6. Verb tense is expressed with time particles before the verb: ua (completed), ke...nei (happening now), e...ana (will happen).

7. Questions are formed by placing the particle 'āhea (when) / wai (who) / heaha (what) / hea (where) at the start of the sentence.

8. The plural is expressed with the particle mau before the noun: mau wa'a = canoes; mau moana = the many oceans.

## Phrases & Expressions

| Phrase | Literal Translation | Usage |
|---|---|---|
| Aloha 'ohana | "Love to the family" | Greeting among crew / kin |
| Ua holo wa'a moana | "The canoe has sailed the deep" | The journey is complete |
| E hoku ana maka | "The stars will guide toward deep water" | Trust your navigation / have faith |
| Mahalo kai nui | "Gratitude to the great sea" | Offering thanks before a voyage |
| Mana 'āina kā | "The power of our land" | This island gives us strength / home empowers |
| Heaha kā hāwai? | "What is our gift?" | What do we bring to offer? (trade opener) |
| Ke nalu nei kona mana | "The waves roll with their power now" | The ocean is in its strength / respect the sea |

## Idioms & Cultural Sayings

| Idiom | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Hoku brak uka — "The star does not go toward shore" | What is fixed and true does not bend to comfort. Stars guide but don't follow you home. |
| Wa'a iki mana nui — "A small canoe with great power" | Never underestimate modest appearances. The most capable people often seem unassuming. |
| Moana 'ohana — "The ocean is family" | The sea is not an enemy to be conquered but a kin to be respected. Navigators speak this before sailing. |
| Nalu kai mahalo — "The wave knows gratitude" | Said ironically of someone ungrateful — even the wave returns to shore after it's received from the land. |
| Holo makani, holo wa'a — "Wind runs, canoe runs" | You must move with what moves. Adapt to the force around you rather than fighting it. |

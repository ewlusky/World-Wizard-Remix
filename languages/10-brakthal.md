## Cultural Context

Brakthal is the language of the Goblin Tinker clans — chaotic, brilliant engineers and alchemists who believe that every broken thing is a design opportunity. Their language reflects this worldview: there are no words for failure or impossibility, only for "works differently than expected." The language has evolved to handle technical description with extreme precision while maintaining a conversational style that sounds almost childlike to outsiders. Compound words can grow to extraordinary lengths.

## Phonology & Grammar Rules

1. Japanese-style syllable structure: strictly CV (consonant-vowel) except for the syllable-final -n. Consonants: k, g, t, d, b, p, m, n, r, s, z, h. Vowels: a, i, u, e, o.

2. German-style compounding: words join to make new words without spaces or hyphens. Brakthal-speakers pride themselves on the length and precision of their compounds. "Zakatubiratokemoshin" = "the-small-fire-that-makes-the-gear-turn-in-the-right-direction-apparatus."

3. Particles follow nouns to indicate grammatical function (subject: -ga; object: -wo; topic: -wa; location: -de; direction: -ni; instrument: -de). These cannot be compounded.

4. Verbs go at the end of the sentence. "The gear -ga the spring -wo turns" = "The gear turns the spring."

5. Tense markers precede the verb: ima (now), mō (already/completed), korekara (from here/in the future), itsumo (always/habitual).

6. Diminutives use the prefix ko- (small): kora (small gear); kobi (small fire). Augmentatives use ō- (great): ōra (great machine), ōbi (great explosion).

7. Technical precision: the suffix -shin means "mechanism/apparatus"; -tome means "control/stop"; -kami means "spirit/intelligence of a thing."

8. Questions end in the particle -ka; strong emphasis uses -zo; polite requests use -ne (gently) or -yo (firmly).

## Phrases & Expressions

| Phrase | Literal Translation | Usage |
|---|---|---|
| Kagura-ga ugoku-ka? | "Does the gear function?" | Does this work? / Is it ready? |
| Omoshiroi mishin-da! | "This machine is interesting!" | It's broken but in a fascinating way |
| Bōku-ga naosu-yo | "I will fix it firmly" | I'm on it / I'll handle this |
| Kowareta kami brak | "There is no not-yet-working spirit" | Every machine has a spirit — it's not broken, just misunderstood |
| Ōbishin-de tobira-wo tomeru-ne? | "Shall we gently stop the door with the great engine?" | Polite request to shut down the main mechanism |
| Ima zakatu ugoku! | "The mechanism works now!" | It works! (Triumphant exclamation) |
| Ko-mishin-ga ōkagura-wo... omoshiroi-da | "The small machine is making the great gear... interesting" | Something has gone interestingly wrong at scale |

## Idioms & Cultural Sayings

| Idiom | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Kowareta brak — "There is no broken" | The core Brakthal worldview: nothing is truly broken, only operating differently. Used as encouragement. |
| Kami ugoku, bōku tomeru — "The spirit moves, I control" | The mark of a master tinker — you work with a machine's nature, not against it. |
| Ōbishin omoshiroi kami — "Great engines have interesting spirits" | Complex systems develop their own logic. Respect the machine. |
| Tobira-wo naosu, tobira-wo kowasu — "To fix the door is to break the door" | Every repair changes the thing. Nothing is restored to exactly what it was. |
| Mishin-ga itsumo bōku-wo oshieru — "The machine always teaches me" | You learn more from your tools than from any teacher. Said humbly by even the greatest engineers. |

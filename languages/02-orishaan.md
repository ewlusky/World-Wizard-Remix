## Cultural Context

The Orishaan are the people of the great river delta — traders, spirit-speakers, and renowned healers. Their language is tonal: the same syllable spoken in a high, mid, or low tone carries different meanings. Orishaan society recognizes four genders, and the language has noun classes (similar to Swahili) that carry spiritual significance: animate things, spirit-touched things, fluid things (water, fire, time), and stone things (unchanging, dead, or divine).

## Phonology & Grammar Rules

1. Orishaan is a tonal language with three tones: High (written with an acute accent: á), Mid (unmarked: a), and Low (written with a grave accent: à). Tone changes meaning entirely — oba (mid-mid) = king; oba (high-low) = spirit; oba (low-high) = river bend.

2. Syllable structure is predominantly CV (consonant-vowel). No consonant clusters — each consonant must be followed by a vowel.

3. Noun classes determine agreement: Animate (prefix o-/e-); Spirit-touched (prefix ori-/iri-); Fluid (prefix omi-/eme-); Stone/Divine (prefix apa-/upa-).

4. Verb-Subject-Object (VSO) word order — the action comes first, then who does it, then what is affected.

5. Plural is formed by reduplication of the first syllable: oba (king) → o-oba (kings).

6. Adjectives are constructed as relative clauses: not "the red drum" but "the drum that is red."

7. Time is expressed not with tense but with aspect particles: ti (completed action), ni (ongoing), ma (habitual), and so (future-intention).

8. Honorifics are prefix-based: Ori- before a name means "honored elder"; Emi- means "beloved child of the spirits."

| Ọkọ̀ tí ò jẹ ẹja rìn — "A canoe that does not carry fish walks" | Empty leadership. Someone with authority and no purpose. |

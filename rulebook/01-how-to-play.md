# How to Play

Leave the map somewhere everyone walks past. When you have a few minutes, take a turn, draw something, write it down. That is the whole game.

## The One-Minute Rules

1. You have **2d6 AP** for this whole Age. Roll once, write it on your sheet.
2. When you take a turn, pick one action. Spend the AP. Draw it on the map. Write it in the Log. Add 1 to the Tally.
3. When your AP runs low, **Pass** to get more, but the world pushes back.
4. When the Tally hits its target, everyone votes whether to end the Age. If yes, run the **Survival Round** and move on.

Everything else is detail you read only when you need it.

## How a Turn Works at the Table

**1. Pick your path.** Either:

- 🎡 **Spin the Wheel** and do the action it lands on, or
- 🎯 **Choose** any action you want.

**2. Roll or decide.** If the action calls for a table, you may:

- roll on the table, or
- pick an entry you like, or
- **make something up.** Always allowed, at every step.

**3. Pay the AP.** Subtract the cost.

**4. Draw it on the map.** It does not have to be pretty. An outline and a label is enough.

**5. Write the note.** One or two sentences in the Log.

**6. Add 1 to the Tally.**

## Why the wheel exists

The wheel is not a randomizer bolted on for variety. It is the entry ramp, and it is load-bearing.

A blank map and an open invitation to invent something is the hardest possible prompt. Some players want it. Others freeze in front of it, and the freeze is not lack of interest — it is the weight of an unbounded choice plus the feeling of being watched while you make it. A player in that position does not need more options. They need the first decision made for them so they can get to the second one, which is the fun one.

So the wheel's segments are the table names. Spin it, and it tells you *what kind of thing* you are adding. Now you are not inventing from nothing, you are answering a much smaller question. Roll on that table if you want a starting point, or ignore the roll and use your own idea, which by then you usually have.

Spin-or-Choose is a real fork, permanently. A player who wants a blank page takes Choose. A player who wants a running start takes the wheel. Neither is the beginner option.

The same reasoning produced two other rules that are easy to mistake for filler:

- **You may skip your turn.** No cost, no penalty, no explanation owed.
- **A turn can be tiny.** See the [Quiet Turn](03-the-ap-economy.md#the-quiet-turn) — adding one detail to something that already exists, or drawing one connection between two things, is a complete and legitimate contribution.

Together these three make the difference between a game that a hesitant player tolerates and one they actually pick up. Do not optimize them away in the name of tightening the loop.

## Building a wheel

Any spinner works. Divide it into segments and label them with the table names you want in rotation — see [`../tables/WHEEL.md`](../tables/WHEEL.md) for the mapping and a suggested segment set per Age. A wheel with 12 segments and a physical click to it beats a digital one, because the point is the ceremony of getting started, not the entropy.
